# Cross-Unit Master Distinction Tables (Differentiate-Between Master List)
## High-Scoring Tabular Answers with Minimum 6 Evaluator Parameters

In university examinations, "Differentiate Between" questions carry 5 to 10 marks and must **always** be presented in a structured table. Every comparison below includes a dedicated **Basis of Distinction / Parameter** column with at least 6 points.

---

### 1. GATT vs WTO (Unit I)

| Basis of Distinction | General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT 1947) | World Trade Organization (WTO 1995) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Legal Nature & Status** | A provisional legal agreement/multilateral treaty with no independent legal personality. | A permanent international intergovernmental organization with recognized legal personality and charter. |
| **2. Institutional Structure** | Had no formal institutional structure; operated via an ad-hoc secretariat and periodic negotiating "Rounds". | Full-fledged institutional structure comprising Ministerial Conference, General Council, and specialized committees. |
| **3. Scope of Coverage** | Regulated trade in **merchandise/tangible goods only**. | Regulates trade in **goods (GATT), services (GATS), and intellectual property rights (TRIPS)**. |
| **4. Dispute Settlement** | Consensus-based dispute settlement where the defending country could veto panel establishment or report adoption. | Binding, quasi-judicial Dispute Settlement Understanding (DSU) featuring **Reverse Consensus** and Appellate Body review. |
| **5. Multilateral Commitments** | Plurilateral approach; member countries could opt out of specific codes (e.g., Tokyo Round codes). | **Single Undertaking** principle; all multilateral agreements are accepted as an indivisible package. |
| **6. Member Designation** | Signatories were formally referred to as "Contracting Parties". | Sovereign nations are formal "Members". |
| **7. Trade Policy Surveillance** | No formal, systemic trade policy review mechanism existed. | Institutionalized **Trade Policy Review Mechanism (TPRM)** conducting periodic audits of members' trade policies. |

### 2. Commercial Invoice vs Proforma Invoice (Unit I & II)

| Basis of Distinction | Proforma Invoice | Commercial Invoice |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Primary Purpose** | A preliminary quotation or formal offer sent by the exporter to initiate the sale or facilitate import licensing. | The definitive financial accounting document and legal bill demanding payment for delivered goods. |
| **2. Time of Issuance** | Issued during the pre-shipment/negotiation stage, before the contract is finalized or production commences. | Issued post-production, after the goods are packed and readied or dispatched for shipment. |
| **3. Legal Enforceability** | Not a legally binding financial claim; serves as an estimate or draft unless accepted as a formal contract. | Legally binding accounting document admissible in a court of law and recognized by tax/customs authorities. |
| **4. Customs & Duty Assessment** | Cannot be used for final customs clearance or duty assessment at the importing port (except for temporary import/samples). | Mandatory primary document used by Customs under Section 14/46/50 of the Customs Act for tariff valuation. |
| **5. Banking & LC Operations** | Used by the importer to open a Letter of Credit (LC) or apply for foreign exchange sanction from their bank. | Presented under Article 18 of UCP 600 to the negotiating bank to claim payment under the Letter of Credit. |
| **6. Contents & Quantities** | Quantities, delivery dates, and total values are estimated or indicative. | Quantities, unit rates, net/gross weights, Incoterms, and total payable amounts are exact and definitive. |

### 3. Clean Bill of Lading vs Claused / Foul Bill of Lading (Unit I & II)

| Basis of Distinction | Clean Bill of Lading | Claused / Foul / Dirty Bill of Lading |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Definition (UCP 600 Art. 27)** | A B/L that bears no clause or notation expressly declaring a defective condition of the goods or packaging. | A B/L that contains explicit carrier notations indicating defective, leaking, torn, or damaged packaging or goods. |
| **2. Standard Carrier Statement** | Contains the standard printed clause: *"Received in apparent good order and condition"* without negative endorsements. | Bears specific annotations such as *"3 drums leaking"*, *"cartons torn and crushed"*, or *"chilled cargo received defrosted"*. |
| **3. LC Negotiability** | **Universally acceptable** by commercial banks for document negotiation under Letters of Credit. | **Rejected immediately** by banks as a non-compliant document/discrepancy unless the LC expressly permits claused B/Ls. |
| **4. Legal Implication for Carrier** | The ocean carrier is strictly liable if the consignee receives damaged cargo at destination. | Relieves the shipping line of liability for pre-existing cargo/package damage specifically noted at load port. |
| **5. Letter of Indemnity (LOI) Risk** | Issued legitimately without questionable side-agreements. | Exporters sometimes wrongfully offer an LOI to shipping lines to issue a clean B/L; courts consider this fraudulent. |
| **6. Marketability & Title** | Freely negotiable document of title commanding immediate commercial value in maritime trade. | Impaired title document; buyers and cargo underwriters will refuse to accept or insure goods under foul B/Ls. |

### 4. Bill of Lading (B/L) vs Air Waybill (AWB) (Unit I)

| Basis of Distinction | Ocean Bill of Lading (B/L) | Air Waybill (AWB) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Mode of Transport** | Maritime shipping (ocean/sea or inland waterways). | International air freight. |
| **2. Document of Title Status** | **Yes**, it is a negotiable document of title representing physical ownership of goods. | **No**, it is strictly a non-negotiable consignment note; it does not confer title to goods. |
| **3. Delivery Mechanism** | Carrier releases cargo at destination port **only upon physical surrender** of an original endorsed B/L. | Carrier releases cargo to the named consignee upon proof of identity **without demanding surrender** of the original AWB. |
| **4. Negotiability / Endorsement** | Can be drawn "to order" and transferred via blank or special endorsement to third parties. | Issued to a named consignee only; cannot be endorsed or negotiated to third parties. |
| **5. Governing International Law** | Hague-Visby Rules, Hamburg Rules, or Indian Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 1925. | Warsaw Convention 1929, Hague Protocol 1955, or Montreal Convention 1999. |
| **6. Number of Originals** | Typically issued in a full set of **three original signed copies** ("3/3 original bills"). | Issued in standard sets (Original 1 for carrier, Original 2 for consignee, Original 3 for shipper). |

### 5. Shipping Bill vs Bill of Entry (Unit I & II)

| Basis of Distinction | Shipping Bill (SB) | Bill of Entry (BE) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Purpose & Transaction** | Document filed by the **Exporter** to obtain customs clearance for exporting goods out of India. | Document filed by the **Importer** to obtain customs clearance for importing foreign goods into India. |
| **2. Governing Section** | Filed under **Section 50** of the Customs Act, 1962. | Filed under **Section 46** of the Customs Act, 1962. |
| **3. Types of Forms** | Dutiable SB (yellow), Duty-Free SB (white), Drawback SB (green), DEPB/Advance Authorisation SB (blue). | Form I (White - Home Consumption), Form II (Yellow - Warehousing/Into-Bond), Form III (Green - Ex-Bond). |
| **4. Final Customs Clearance Order** | Results in the issuance of **Let Export Order (LEO)** under Section 51. | Results in the issuance of **Out of Charge (OOC)** under Section 47. |
| **5. Assessment & Tax Treatment** | Used to verify export prohibition/restriction and claim export incentives (RoDTEP, Duty Drawback). | Used to calculate and levy Basic Customs Duty (BCD), Social Welfare Surcharge (SWS), and IGST. |
| **6. Integration with RBI Ledgers** | Electronically transmitted from ICEGATE to RBI's **EDPMS** to track inward remittance realization. | Electronically transmitted from ICEGATE to RBI's **IDPMS** to reconcile outward foreign exchange payments. |

### 6. Documentary Collection: D/P (Sight) vs D/A (Usance) (Unit II)

| Basis of Distinction | Documents Against Payment (D/P) | Documents Against Acceptance (D/A) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Payment Timing** | Importer must pay cash/remittance **at sight** before receiving title documents. | Importer obtains title documents immediately upon signing/accepting a **time (usance) draft**. |
| **2. Release of Documents** | Collecting bank releases original B/L and documents only after full monetary settlement. | Collecting bank releases documents upon formal legal acceptance of the draft (promising payment in 30/60/90 days). |
| **3. Financial Risk to Exporter** | **Low to Moderate:** Exporter retains legal control of cargo until payment is secured. | **High:** Exporter relinquishes cargo control before receiving actual payment. |
| **4. Importer Default Consequence** | If the importer refuses payment, the cargo remains at the destination port; exporter can re-route or recall cargo. | If the importer defaults at maturity, the exporter holds only an accepted bill of exchange and must initiate legal action. |
| **5. Financial Facility for Importer** | Importer receives no supplier trade credit; must finance the purchase upfront. | Importer enjoys extended credit financing, enabling them to sell the cargo in the domestic market to fund the payment. |
| **6. Governing ICC Framework** | Governed by **Uniform Rules for Collections (URC 522)**, Article 7. | Governed by **Uniform Rules for Collections (URC 522)**, Article 7. |

---

### 7. Letter of Credit (LC) vs Documentary Collection (Unit II)

| Basis of Distinction | Letter of Credit (UCP 600) | Documentary Collection (URC 522) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Bank's Primary Role** | Issuing Bank provides an **independent, irrevocable payment guarantee** to the exporter. | Remitting and Collecting Banks act strictly as **fiduciary processing agents** with zero payment undertaking. |
| **2. Source of Payment Security** | Creditworthiness and balance sheet strength of the **Issuing/Confirming Bank**. | Creditworthiness and integrity of the **Buyer/Importer** alone. |
| **3. Cost & Bank Charges** | Significantly higher fees (opening commissions, advising fees, amendment fees, confirmation charges). | Low administrative charges (handling and collection commission only). |
| **4. Governing Legal Rules** | ICC Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits (**UCP 600**). | ICC Uniform Rules for Collections (**URC 522**). |
| **5. Document Scrutiny Standard** | Banks must examine documents with **strict compliance** within 5 banking days (Art. 14). | Banks verify only that the documents received match the collection schedule; they do not examine for discrepancies. |
| **6. Suitability** | High-risk markets, new or untested buyers, politically unstable nations, high-value custom machinery. | Low-to-moderate risk markets, established buyer relationships, open-credit competitive environments. |

---

### 8. Revocable vs Irrevocable Letter of Credit (Unit II)

| Basis of Distinction | Revocable Letter of Credit | Irrevocable Letter of Credit |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Modification or Cancellation** | Can be amended or cancelled by the Issuing Bank at any moment **without prior notice** to the beneficiary. | Cannot be amended or cancelled without the **express agreement** of the Issuing Bank, Confirming Bank, and Beneficiary. |
| **2. Exporter's Security** | Negligible security; buyer can instruct the bank to cancel the credit while goods are already in high seas transit. | Absolute security; exporter is legally guaranteed payment provided conforming documents are tendered. |
| **3. Current UCP 600 Status** | **Obsolete.** UCP 600 removed all articles concerning revocable credits. | **Default rule.** Under Article 3 of UCP 600, a credit is deemed irrevocable even if it contains no statement to that effect. |
| **4. Acceptance in International Trade** | Never accepted in professional cross-border transactions or by export credit agencies. | Universally accepted as standard commercial banking instruments. |
| **5. Bank Liability** | Bank is liable only for payments negotiated prior to receipt of formal cancellation notice. | Issuing bank has an unconditional, continuous legal liability from the moment of credit issuance. |
| **6. Collateral Value for Finance** | Commercial banks will **never grant pre-shipment packing credit** against a revocable LC. | Indian commercial banks readily sanction pre-shipment export packing credit against an irrevocable LC. |

### 9. Confirmed vs Unconfirmed Letter of Credit (Unit II)

| Basis of Distinction | Confirmed Letter of Credit | Unconfirmed Letter of Credit |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Number of Bank Undertakings** | **Two independent guarantees:** The Issuing Bank PLUS the Confirming Bank (usually in the exporter's country). | **Single undertaking:** The Issuing Bank alone guarantees payment; the advising bank merely notifies. |
| **2. Country & Sovereign Risk** | Insulates the exporter against political, economic, or foreign exchange transfer freezes in the buyer's country. | Exporter remains fully exposed to sovereign default, political turmoil, or FX illiquidity in the buyer's country. |
| **3. Presentation of Documents** | Exporter presents documents to the Confirming Bank domestically and receives payment upon compliance. | Documents must ultimately be forwarded to the overseas Issuing Bank for honor and settlement. |
| **4. Additional Cost** | Incurs an additional confirmation fee (0.25% to 2%+ per quarter depending on country risk rating). | No confirmation fee; only standard advising and handling charges apply. |
| **5. Governing Article** | Governed under **Article 8 of UCP 600** (Liability of a Confirming Bank). | Governed under **Article 7 of UCP 600** (Liability of an Issuing Bank). |
| **6. Trade Situation Mandate** | Essential when exporting to politically volatile regions, sanctioned nations, or financially distressed banks. | Standard between mature economies or when the Issuing Bank is an established top-tier global financial institution. |

### 10. FOB vs CIF (Incoterms 2020) (Unit II)

| Basis of Distinction | FOB (Free on Board) | CIF (Cost, Insurance and Freight) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Freight Obligation** | **Buyer** contracts and pays for ocean carriage from the named port of shipment to destination. | **Seller** contracts and pays for maritime freight to the named port of destination. |
| **2. Marine Insurance Obligation** | **Buyer** is responsible for procuring marine cargo insurance to protect their goods in transit. | **Seller** is legally obligated to procure marine insurance at their own cost (minimum ICC 'C' cover). |
| **3. Transfer of Risk** | Transferred when goods are safely **loaded on board the vessel** at the named port of shipment. | Transferred at the **exact same point** as FOB: when goods are loaded on board the vessel at shipment port. |
| **4. Transfer of Costs** | Seller pays costs up to loading on board; buyer pays all subsequent freight, unloading, and import duties. | Seller pays all costs including ocean freight and insurance premium up to the destination port. |
| **5. Export Customs Clearance** | Seller clears goods for export at the origin customs port. | Seller clears goods for export at the origin customs port. |
| **6. Mode of Transport Suitability** | Strictly restricted to **maritime and inland waterway transport** (not intended for container freight). | Strictly restricted to **maritime and inland waterway transport** (not intended for container freight). |

---

### 11. CIF vs CIP (Incoterms 2020) (Unit II)

| Basis of Distinction | CIF (Cost, Insurance and Freight) | CIP (Carriage and Insurance Paid to) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Applicable Modes of Transport** | **Maritime and inland waterway transport only** (bulk commodities, oil, minerals). | **Any mode of transport** or multimodal carriage (air freight, road, rail, containerized sea freight). |
| **2. Mandatory Insurance Coverage** | Requires minimum coverage: **Institute Cargo Clauses (C)** (ICC C) unless agreed otherwise. | Requires maximum "All Risks" coverage: **Institute Cargo Clauses (A)** (ICC A) under Incoterms 2020. |
| **3. Point of Risk Handover** | When goods are safely **loaded on board the vessel** at the port of departure. | When goods are **handed over to the first carrier** nominated by the seller at the point of origin. |
| **4. Terminal Container Suitability** | Unsuitable for modern container logistics (container sits at CFS/terminal before ship loading). | Highly recommended by ICC for all containerized shipments (FCL/LCL). |
| **5. Document of Transport** | Traditional Ocean Bill of Lading (B/L). | Multimodal Transport Document, Air Waybill (AWB), Truck CMR, or Rail Consignment Note. |
| **6. Incoterms 2020 Revision Impact** | Left unaltered at minimum ICC (C) level to satisfy historical bulk commodity trade conventions. | Elevated default insurance cover requirement from ICC (C) to **ICC (A)**. |

---

### 12. EXW vs DDP (Incoterms 2020) (Unit II)

| Basis of Distinction | EXW (Ex Works) | DDP (Delivered Duty Paid) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Polar Extremes of Spectrum** | Represents the **minimum obligation** for the seller in the Incoterms framework. | Represents the **maximum obligation** for the seller in the Incoterms framework. |
| **2. Export Customs Formalities** | Buyer must clear goods for export in seller's country; seller has zero customs responsibility. | Seller must handle all export customs clearance, export declarations, and terminal fees. |
| **3. Import Customs & Duties** | Buyer handles import clearance and pays all destination tariffs, duties, and GST. | **Seller** must handle import customs clearance, pay all destination import duties, clearance fees, and taxes. |
| **4. Physical Delivery Point** | At seller's factory/warehouse premises (seller is not even obliged to load goods onto buyer's truck). | At buyer's designated destination facility or warehouse, unloaded from transport. |
| **5. Risk Transfer Location** | Transferred when goods are placed at the buyer's disposal at the seller's factory floor. | Transferred only when goods arrive at the destination premises ready for unloading. |
| **6. Practical Feasibility** | Creates compliance hurdles for foreign buyers unfamiliar with domestic tax/export laws. | Exceedingly risky for sellers who lack a physical legal presence or tax registration in the importing country. |

### 13. Container Freight Station (CFS) vs Inland Container Depot (ICD) (Unit II)

| Basis of Distinction | Container Freight Station (CFS) | Inland Container Depot (ICD) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Physical Location** | Located in close geographical proximity to a **gateway sea port** (normally within 10–50 km). | Located deep in the **hinterland / inland industrial centers** (e.g., Tughlakabad-Delhi, Moradabad, Ludhiana). |
| **2. Autonomous Dry Port Status** | Functions primarily as an operational extension and cargo consolidation node of the parent seaport. | Functions as an **independent Dry Port** with its own distinct customs station code and multimodal railhead. |
| **3. Primary Operational Focus** | Stuffing, stripping, and consolidating **LCL (Less than Container Load)** cargo consignments. | Handling both **FCL (Full Container Load)** and LCL cargo, with direct rail-flat connectivity to coastal gateway ports. |
| **4. Customs Jurisdiction** | Operates under the direct administrative jurisdiction of the maritime Commissioner of Customs. | Operates under an independent Commissioner of Customs assigned to that inland dry port territory. |
| **5. Intermodal Transit Bonding** | Cargo transfers between CFS and seaport take place via bonded road trailers under local transit escort. | Containers move between ICD and seaports under **bond and bank guarantee via dedicated container trains (CONCOR)**. |
| **6. Multi-Modal Bill of Lading** | Shipping lines rarely issue an inland Multi-Modal B/L showing CFS as the original place of receipt. | Shipping lines regularly issue a **Combined Transport / Multimodal Bill of Lading** naming the ICD as place of receipt. |

### 14. FCL (Full Container Load) vs LCL (Less than Container Load) (Unit II)

| Basis of Distinction | Full Container Load (FCL) | Less than Container Load (LCL) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Consignment Volume** | Cargo volume is sufficient to fill or justify the exclusive commercial use of an entire 20ft or 40ft container. | Cargo volume is too small to fill a full container; must be consolidated with goods from other shippers. |
| **2. Shippers & Consignees** | Typically **one single shipper** sending cargo to **one single consignee**. | **Multiple unrelated shippers** consolidated into one container, destined for **multiple consignees**. |
| **3. Stuffing & De-stuffing Venue** | Done at the exporter’s factory (*Factory Stuffing*) or at port/ICD under customs supervision. | Must take place at a **Container Freight Station (CFS)** by an authorized freight consolidator / NVOCC. |
| **4. Risk of Transit Damage/Pilferage** | Low risk; container is sealed with a high-security bottle seal at origin and opened only at final destination. | High risk; multiple physical handlings during consolidation/de-consolidation increase risk of cargo damage or taint. |
| **5. Ocean Freight Charging Metric** | Charged on a flat rate per container (e.g., USD 1,800 per 40ft High Cube Container). | Charged based on **Freight Ton (W/M - Weight or Measurement)**, whichever generates higher revenue. |
| **6. Customs Clearance Delay** | Independent; customs examination of the container affects only that specific exporter's shipment. | Interdependent; if customs detains one suspicious package inside an LCL container, the entire container is held up. |

### 15. Traditional Central Excise vs Modern GST Export Clearance (Unit II)

| Basis of Distinction | Traditional Central Excise Regime (Pre-July 2017) | Modern Goods & Services Tax (GST) Regime |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Governing Legislation** | Central Excise Act, 1944 and Central Excise Rules, 2002. | Central Goods and Services Tax (CGST) Act, 2017 & IGST Act, 2017 (Section 16). |
| **2. Primary Export Removal Form** | **Form ARE-1** (Application for Removal of Excisable Goods for Export) in multiple colored copies. | Form ARE-1 is **completely abolished**; electronic **Shipping Bill** on ICEGATE serves as primary export document. |
| **3. Mechanism Without Tax Payment** | Export under **B-1 Bond** with bank guarantee or Letter of Undertaking (LUT); raw materials via **Form CT-1**. | Export under **online LUT (Letter of Undertaking)** filed annually on the GST portal without bank guarantee. |
| **4. Duty Refund / Rebate Route** | **Rebate of Duty (Rule 18):** Pay central excise duty on finished goods, claim refund from Maritime Commissioner. | **Payment of IGST Route:** Pay IGST via monthly return; electronic refund is processed automatically through ICEGATE. |
| **5. Tax Classification of Exports** | Treated as "Exempted Goods" or subject to specific excise rebate notifications. | Classified as **"Zero Rated Supplies"** under Section 16 of the IGST Act, allowing full Input Tax Credit (ITC) refund. |
| **6. Administrative Agency** | Central Excise Range Superintendent and Maritime Commissioner of Central Excise. | Integrated electronic linkage between **GSTN (GST Network)** and **CBIC/ICEGATE**; no physical visits to tax offices. |

### 16. Pre-Shipment Finance vs Post-Shipment Finance (Unit III)

| Basis of Distinction | Pre-Shipment Finance (Packing Credit) | Post-Shipment Finance |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Stage of Credit Lifecycle** | Extended **before** the shipment of goods takes place (manufacturing & procurement phase). | Extended **after** the goods have been cleared by customs and loaded on board the carrier. |
| **2. Primary Objective** | Working capital to purchase raw materials, process, manufacture, pack, test, and transport goods to port. | Bridging liquidity gap during the credit period extended to the overseas buyer (waiting for bill maturity). |
| **3. Required Primary Documents** | Confirmed Export Order or Irrevocable Letter of Credit, valid IEC, and RCMC. | Commercial Invoice, Packing List, **Shipping Bill (LEO copy)**, and Original Ocean Bill of Lading / Air Waybill. |
| **4. Modes of Advance** | Extended as Pre-shipment Export Packing Credit in Rupees (EPC) or Foreign Currency (PCFC). | Export Bill Purchase (EBP), Export Bill Discounting (EBD), or Advance Against Bills Sent on Collection. |
| **5. Liquidation Mechanism** | Liquidated out of post-shipment credit proceeds or direct proceeds of the export bill discounting. | Liquidated upon **realization and repatriation** of export sale proceeds from the foreign buyer via AD bank. |
| **6. Risk Profile for Banks** | Higher operational risk: Exporter may fail to manufacture goods, default on quality, or cancel shipment. | Lower risk: Goods are physically manufactured, inspected, customs cleared, and shipped in transit. |

### 17. Rupee Packing Credit vs PCFC (Packing Credit in Foreign Currency) (Unit III)

| Basis of Distinction | Rupee Pre-Shipment Credit (EPC) | Packing Credit in Foreign Currency (PCFC) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Currency of Disbursement** | Disbursed in **Indian Rupees (INR)** into the exporter's domestic current/credit account. | Disbursed in **freely convertible foreign currencies** (USD, EUR, GBP, JPY). |
| **2. Benchmark Interest Rate** | Linked to domestic benchmark lending rates (MCLR / Repo-linked rate) set by Indian commercial banks. | Linked to international benchmark rates (e.g., **Term SOFR** - Secured Overnight Financing Rate) plus spread. |
| **3. Effective Cost of Borrowing** | Substantially higher nominal interest rate (typically 8.5% to 11% p.a., mitigated partly by interest subvention). | Substantially lower nominal interest rate (typically SOFR + 150 to 250 bps, yielding ~5.5% to 6.5% p.a.). |
| **4. Foreign Exchange Risk** | Exporter is exposed to rupee fluctuation risk between procurement date and eventual export realization date. | **Natural hedge:** Exporter borrows in USD and repays directly from incoming export proceeds in USD; zero FX conversion loss. |
| **5. Import Component Utilization** | Incurs currency conversion friction and loss if raw materials must be imported from abroad. | Highly cost-effective for import-intensive exporters to pay overseas raw material suppliers directly in foreign exchange. |
| **6. Availability & Thresholds** | Readily available to all exporters including small MSMEs from any commercial bank branch. | Generally restricted by banks to creditworthy exporters with proven international order books and foreign currency accounts. |

### 18. Factoring vs Forfaiting (Unit III)

| Basis of Distinction | Export Factoring | International Forfaiting |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Credit Tenure** | Restricted to **short-term receivables** (typically up to 90–180 days). | Accommodates **medium-to-long term receivables** (typically 1 to 7 years). |
| **2. Recourse Structure** | Available on both **Recourse and Non-Recourse** basis (recourse is common in developing markets). | Operates **strictly on a 100% Non-Recourse basis**; forfaiter assumes all credit and political risk. |
| **3. Nature of Underlying Goods** | Consumer goods, apparel, agricultural items, and standard industrial raw materials. | High-value capital goods, heavy machinery, turn-key industrial projects, and aircraft/vessels. |
| **4. Financial Instrument Used** | Financed against assignment of open account **Commercial Invoices** and book debts. | Financed against negotiable debt instruments: **Promissory Notes or Usance Bills of Exchange with Bank Aval**. |
| **5. Advance Financing Percentage** | Factor advances **80% to 90%** of invoice value upfront; balance 10–20% retained as reserve until collection. | Forfaiter provides **100% financing** (less the pre-calculated discount margin for the entire multi-year tenure). |
| **6. Secondary Market Trading** | Factored receivables cannot be traded or transferred on secondary financial markets. | Forfaited paper (avalised notes) can be freely bought and sold on international secondary debt markets. |

---

### 19. Recourse Factoring vs Non-Recourse Factoring (Unit III)

| Basis of Distinction | Recourse Factoring | Non-Recourse Factoring |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Credit Risk Allocation** | The **Exporter** bears the ultimate bad debt risk if the foreign buyer defaults or goes bankrupt. | The **Factor** bears 100% of the bad debt credit loss if the foreign buyer becomes financially insolvent. |
| **2. Action on Buyer Default** | If buyer fails to pay at maturity, the factor exercises recourse and **demands full refund** of advance from exporter. | If buyer fails to pay due to financial default, the factor absorbs the loss and cannot recover the advance from exporter. |
| **3. Factoring Fee Structure** | Lower commission fee (factor charges primarily for ledger administration, collection, and financing). | Higher service commission (reflects credit insurance premium and risk underwriting exposure). |
| **4. Exporter Balance Sheet Impact** | Reflected as a contingent liability or borrowing on the exporter's balance sheet. | Off-balance sheet financing; book debts are permanently removed from the exporter's balance sheet. |
| **5. Factor's Credit Vetting** | Factor evaluates the financial standing of both the exporter and the foreign buyer. | Factor conducts exhaustive, stringent credit vetting solely on the overseas buyer before approving credit limits. |
| **6. Dispute Handling Exception** | Irrelevant to recourse status (exporter must repay regardless of reason for non-payment). | Factor’s risk cover applies to financial insolvency only; if buyer refuses payment due to commercial disputes (quality defect), recourse reverts to exporter. |

### 20. General Average vs Particular Average (Unit III)

| Basis of Distinction | General Average (GA) | Particular Average (PA) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Nature of the Peril/Loss** | **Intentional and voluntary sacrifice or expenditure** deliberately incurred in time of imminent maritime peril. | **Accidental, fortuitous partial loss or damage** caused directly by an insured maritime peril (e.g., storm, fire). |
| **2. Objective of the Act** | Committed for the **common safety and preservation** of the entire maritime adventure (ship, cargo, and freight). | Affects a specific cargo interest solely through misfortune; no element of common preservation. |
| **3. Governing Legal Doctrine** | Governed universally by the **York-Antwerp Rules** (incorporated into maritime bills of lading). | Governed by the **Marine Insurance Act, 1963** and Institute Cargo Clauses. |
| **4. Loss Sharing / Allocation** | Shared **proportionally by all surviving interests** (shipowner, cargo owners, and freight earner). | Borne **solely by the owner** of the specific damaged cargo (or their cargo insurance company). |
| **5. Classic Trade Example** | Jettisoning 100 containers overboard to lighten a grounded vessel and prevent it from breaking apart. | Seawater enters a cargo hold during a severe typhoon, soaking and ruining 100 bags of basmati rice. |
| **6. Release of Surviving Cargo** | Surviving cargo is held under a **Maritime Lien**; owner must sign a GA Bond and provide a bank/insurance guarantee before cargo is released. | Unaffected cargo is released immediately at destination port without lien or contribution demands. |

### 21. Marine Cargo Clauses: ICC (A) vs ICC (B) vs ICC (C) (Unit III)

| Basis of Distinction | Institute Cargo Clauses (C) | Institute Cargo Clauses (B) | Institute Cargo Clauses (A) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Scope of Coverage** | **Named Perils (Minimum):** Major maritime casualties only. | **Named Perils (Intermediate):** Adds specified natural and water damage perils. | **"All Risks":** Covers all accidental loss/damage except specifically excluded perils. |
| **2. Marine Disasters** | Fire, explosion, stranding, grounding, sinking, capsizing, collision, overturning. | Covers all ICC (C) perils PLUS earthquake, volcanic eruption, and lightning. | Covers all ICC (C) and ICC (B) perils plus all unlisted fortuitous transit accidents. |
| **3. Water Ingress & Washing** | **Excluded.** No cover for seawater or river water entering the cargo hold. | **Covered:** Entry of sea/lake/river water into hold; washing overboard by waves. | **Covered:** Comprehensive protection against rain, seawater, condensation, and sweat damage. |
| **4. Sling & Loading Loss** | **Excluded.** Total loss of sling or package dropped during loading/discharge is not covered. | **Covered:** Total loss of any package lost overboard or dropped during loading or unloading. | **Covered:** Any loading, unloading, or handling damage during the entire transit chain. |
| **5. Theft, Pilferage & Non-Delivery** | **Strictly Excluded.** | **Strictly Excluded.** | **Covered** (unless caused by willful misconduct of the insured). |
| **6. Incoterms 2020 Default Rule** | Default mandatory minimum cover required for **CIF** contracts. | Not mandated as default by Incoterms; selected by mutual commercial agreement. | Default mandatory minimum cover required for **CIP** contracts under Incoterms 2020. |

### 22. Demurrage vs Detention (Unit II)

| Basis of Distinction | Demurrage | Detention |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Nature of the Charge** | Financial penalty levied for holding a container/cargo **inside the port/terminal/CFS premises** beyond allowed free days. | Financial penalty levied for holding the shipping line's **container outside the port terminal** beyond allowed free days. |
| **2. Physical Location of Cargo** | Incurred while cargo/container rests within the port authority, terminal yard, or CFS storage boundaries. | Incurred while the container is in the importer's/exporter's factory custody or in transit on a truck outside port premises. |
| **3. Beneficiary of the Charge** | Paid primarily to the **Port Authority / Terminal Operator** (or shipping line if line-demurrage applies). | Paid exclusively to the **Shipping Line / Ocean Carrier** for depriving them of the commercial use of their container equipment. |
| **4. Typical Free Time Period** | Usually **3 to 7 calendar days** from container discharge from vessel. | Usually **5 to 14 calendar days** from container gate-out from terminal to gate-in of empty container. |
| **5. Primary Cause of Incurrence** | Delays in customs clearance, customs dispute, document mismatch, late filing of Bill of Entry, or duty payment delay. | Unloading delays at importer's factory, transport strikes, or delay in trucking the empty container back to the designated depot. |
| **6. Mitigation Strategy** | Pre-filing Bill of Entry via ICEGATE under advance filing rules, using RMS green-channel clearance, prompt duty settlement. | Negotiating extended free days (e.g., 14–21 days) with the shipping line in the freight contract prior to booking. |

### 23. Consignment-Wise Inspection vs Self-Certification Scheme (Unit III)

| Basis of Distinction | Consignment-Wise Inspection | Self-Certification Scheme |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Nature of Verification** | **Physical inspection and sampling** of every individual export shipment batch prior to dispatch. | **System audit approach**; government recognizes exporter’s proven internal quality management system. |
| **2. Institutional Authority** | Officers from the **Export Inspection Agency (EIA)** or approved private agencies conduct physical sampling. | The manufacturer-exporter themselves issue the formal statutory inspection certificate. |
| **3. Operational Turnaround Time** | High delay: Exporter must submit application, wait for inspector visit, await lab test results, and receive certificate. | Zero delay: Exporter packs and dispatches cargo immediately with their own self-generated certificate. |
| **4. Cost of Inspection** | Consignment inspection fee must be paid on every export shipment (historically 0.2%–0.4% of FOB value). | No recurring per-shipment inspection fees payable to the agency. |
| **5. Eligibility Criteria** | Open to all exporters, including beginners, merchant exporters, and irregular shippers. | Granted strictly to established manufacturing units with ISO 9001/HACCP, modern in-house lab, and zero defect history. |
| **6. Degree of Trust & Surveillance** | Low institutional trust; assumes every export batch requires external state verification before leaving India. | High institutional trust; backed by surprise periodic audit checks by EIA quality surveillance teams. |

### 24. WCO 6-Digit HS Code vs Indian 8-Digit ITC(HS) Code (Unit III)

| Basis of Distinction | WCO Harmonized System (6-Digit) | Indian ITC(HS) Code (8-Digit) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Administering Authority** | **World Customs Organization (WCO)**, based in Brussels, Belgium. | **Directorate General of Foreign Trade (DGFT)** & CBIC (Customs Tariff Act, 1975). |
| **2. Global Uniformity** | **Universally standardized** across all 200+ member countries of the WCO (representing 98% of world trade). | Unique to **India**; tailored for domestic economic policy, customs duties, and export-import policy monitoring. |
| **3. Structural Breakdown** | 6 Digits: Chapter (2 digits) + Heading (2 digits) + Subheading (2 digits). | 8 Digits: First 6 digits match WCO HS + Digits 7 & 8 represent **National Tariff Item / Regional Subheading**. |
| **4. Granularity / Specificity** | Broad product categorization (e.g., 1006.30: Semi-milled or wholly milled rice). | High product specificity (e.g., 1006.30.20 specifically identifies *Basmati Rice*). |
| **5. Tariff & Incentive Application** | Cannot be used directly to assess country-specific import duties, GST rates, or export cesses. | Directly linked to Indian Basic Customs Duty (BCD), IGST, Export Duty, and export remission schemes (RoDTEP/RoSCTL). |
| **6. Statutory Requirement in India** | Quoting only 6 digits on Indian customs Shipping Bills or Bills of Entry leads to system rejection on ICEGATE. | Mandatory statutory requirement for all commercial customs declarations in India. |
